01 / The question

What counts as a hot summer day?

We made the definition first, before looking at any results. Summer means June, July and August. A hot day means a daily maximum temperature strictly above 35°C.

The analysis compares the probability of a hot day given it is summer, not the probability that any random calendar day is hot. All comparisons use the same threshold and months.

04 / Our method

Uncertainty is part of the answer.

We use descriptive probability methods with predeclared decisions, not machine learning, predictions or optimized thresholds.

01

Count what actually happened

P(hot | summer, period)

For each 30-year period, divide days with a maximum above 35°C by all nonmissing daily summer maxima. Count missing dates separately; never fill them.

02

Resample entire summers

10,000 × year-block bootstrap

Resample 30 whole summer-year blocks with replacement inside each period. Report each observed rate and both temperature shifts together with a 95% range.

03

Shuffle the year labels

10,000 × shuffled-year reference

Exchange 30-year group labels across 60 summer years. Compare the observed rate difference to the no-period-difference histogram. Keep the fixed random seeds.

07 / How sure can we be?

What the data cannot tell us.

“We cannot tell” is a valid finding. We do not turn uncertainty into a definite claim.

ERA5 is a gridded reconstruction, not a local station measurement. Its spatial resolution can smooth extreme daytime temperatures.
If above-35°C days are rare, the confidence ranges may be wide; an all-zero resample does not prove zero underlying risk.
Missing dates and missing maximums are counted, not interpolated or quietly called non-hot days.
Year-block resampling respects within-summer clustering but does not remove every possible year-to-year dependency.
Permuting year labels gives a chance reference under exchangeability; it does not predict future weather.
Wikipedia monthly normals may use other stations or years, so unmatched periods are not valid evidence of warming.
The point of the project

Not just what changed.
How certain are we?

Every conclusion must be supported by the observed period comparison and an honest uncertainty range. The final statement is left open until the data has been verified.
